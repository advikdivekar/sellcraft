SellCraft AI — complete project blueprint
Tagline: One product photo. A few facts. A complete marketing kit.
SellCraft AI is an AI-powered content creation tool for small online sellers. A seller uploads product photos, enters a few verified details, chooses a platform and creative style, and receives a complete product listing plus a short video advertisement with optional AI voiceover.
The goal is to solve one repetitive, everyday business problem: small sellers spend too much time turning the same product information into good marketing content for different platforms.
The project fits the Side Hustle challenge because it targets a specific customer, produces something they can use immediately, and can use an open-weight language model as a core component.
1. Who are we building for?









Primary customer: Instagram sellers
Independent sellers of jewelry, accessories, gifts, handmade crafts, and home decor who repeatedly create product captions and promotional posts.











Secondary customers: Etsy and local product businesses
Sellers who need detailed listings, keywords, descriptions, promotional creatives, and reusable product content.



The first user should be one real seller with several products to list. They can test whether the generated copy is accurate, whether the video represents the product properly, and whether the result saves them time.
2. The complete user journey

Stage 1 — Add the product
Upload one or more product photos. Enter the product name, price, materials or ingredients, dimensions, features, benefits, and other facts. Fields should be optional where appropriate; do not invent missing information.




Stage 2 — Choose the creative direction
Choose Instagram, Facebook, Pinterest, Etsy, or Shopify. Select the writing tone, visual style, intended audience, and marketing objective.




Stage 3 — Generate the content
An open-weight language model creates platform-specific product copy and a structured video-ad brief. The video pipeline creates a 10-second MP4, and ElevenLabs optionally creates the voiceover.




Stage 4 — Edit and export
The seller reviews the results, edits or regenerates individual sections, previews the video, and downloads the finished assets.

7. What makes the open-AI part meaningful?
The product should not be merely a frontend calling a closed AI API for every task.
The open-weight language model should actually control the core content generation: it interprets the product facts, follows the seller's creative choices, generates platform-specific copy, and produces a structured brief for video creation. Using a model adapter also makes it possible to change models later.
Be transparent in the README and challenge submission:
- Which model generates the product copy.
- Whether the model runs locally or through hosted inference.
- Whether product photos are sent to an external video provider.
- Whether ElevenLabs is used for narration.
- Which components cost money and which have local fallbacks.
Open weights do not automatically mean zero cost or unrestricted commercial licensing. Likewise, if the video provider is hosted, don't claim the entire pipeline runs offline.

Required MVP

- Choose a platform, tone, visual style, and marketing objective.
- Generate structured product copy with an open-weight model.
- Edit and regenerate copy.
- Generate a 10-second MP4 through an available video provider, or a clearly labelled animated-photo fallback.
- Optionally add ElevenLabs narration.
- Preview, download, and copy the outputs.

- Include setup instructions and a real demonstration.
Do not build yet: user accounts, payment processing, automatic publishing to social platforms, a custom-trained model, analytics dashboards, a full product catalog manager, or ten different video generation modes.
Once a real seller confirms that the core workflow is useful, we can expand it.

5. Open-weight language-model integration
Create a dedicated backend service for language-model operations.
Use Ollama with a configurable open-weight model. Prefer a Gemma instruction model as the initial default, but allow a user or developer to change the model with configuration.
Do not hard-code model-specific response assumptions throughout the application.
The service should:
- Check whether Ollama is reachable.
- Check whether the configured model is available.
- Generate structured content.
- Parse and validate responses.
- Retry or repair malformed structured output when reasonable.
- Return understandable errors if the model is offline.

Keep the model provider behind a small interface so another compatible open-weight model can be substituted later.

6. Video-generation subsystem
Treat video generation as an independent backend service. Do not run heavy video inference in a Next.js request handler.
Create a provider abstraction such as:
- ImageToVideoProvider
- OpenWeightImageToVideoProvider
- AnimatedPhotoFallbackProvider
The provider must accept:
- An input product image.
- A motion prompt.
- Desired duration.
- Desired aspect ratio.
- An output location or job context.
It must return a validated media path or a clear error.
Preferred real image-to-video mode
Support a genuine image-to-video model when an actual inference endpoint or compatible local model environment is configured.
LTX-Video/LTX-2 can be evaluated first. Before claiming the integration works, verify the actual model version, weights, licensing terms, invocation method, hardware requirements, and output format.
Do not invent an unofficial API contract. Implement the adapter for the real runtime or inference endpoint being used, and document its configuration.
The motion prompt should:
- Describe camera movement and subtle scene motion.
- Keep the product's defining appearance as consistent as possible.
- Avoid creating fake labels, fake logos, or invented product details.
- Avoid changes that materially misrepresent the product.
Do not ask the image-to-video model to render important titles or precise CTA text. Add typography during the final assembly step.
Animated-photo fallback
When no compatible image-to-video runtime is configured, create a legitimate 10-second MP4 from the uploaded product image using FFmpeg.
Support:
- Subtle zoom and pan.
- Appropriate crop and framing.
- A clean background or solid-color canvas when appropriate.
- Professionally placed text overlays.
- A hook, one verified feature, and a CTA.
- Optional narration audio.
- Captions/subtitles if available.
The fallback must be visually polished, not a raw still image placed inside a video container.
The UI and API response must clearly label this output as an animated-photo ad, not AI-generated motion footage.
Both the real model and fallback paths should produce an actual playable MP4 file with a verified duration of approximately 10 seconds.
Use FFprobe or equivalent validation to check the final file and fail the job if export fails.

8. Final video assembly
Use FFmpeg to assemble the final video.
The assembly pipeline should:
1. Receive the generated footage or animated-photo clip.
2. Normalize duration, dimensions, frame rate, and codec as needed.
3. Add accurate, readable text overlays.
4. Add captions or subtitles when appropriate.
5. Mix in the generated narration if available.
6. Add background audio only when it is licensed or otherwise appropriate for use.
7. Normalize audio levels.
8. Export an MP4 with H.264 video and a widely supported audio codec when audio is present.
9. Validate output duration and media readability.
10. Return a downloadable asset.
Default to vertical 9:16 output, but keep dimensions configurable.
Do not stretch source images incorrectly. Maintain a good visual composition and avoid placing important text too close to screen edges.
Use a real available font and robust text-rendering approach. Handle punctuation and text escaping safely in FFmpeg commands.
Never construct shell commands unsafely from user input.

9. Backend API and job management
Implement a clean API. Names can be adapted to the repository, but the equivalent functionality is required.
Suggested endpoints:
- GET /api/health — basic service status.
- GET /api/providers/status — status of configured providers without exposing secrets.
- POST /api/generations — validate inputs and create a generation job.
- GET /api/generations/{job_id} — return job state and available results.
- POST /api/generations/{job_id}/regenerate-copy — regenerate selected text using updated settings.
- POST /api/generations/{job_id}/regenerate-voiceover — regenerate narration when configured.
- GET /api/generations/{job_id}/assets/{asset_id} — securely retrieve a generated asset.
Use typed request and response models.
A job should have a state such as:
- queued
- generating_copy
- generating_video
- generating_voiceover
- assembling_video
- completed
- failed
Not every stage will run for every request, so voiceover-disabled jobs should skip that stage cleanly.
Persist enough job metadata to recover the results in local development. Do not put the generated MP4's binary contents into SQLite.
Use IDs rather than accepting arbitrary filesystem paths from clients.
Ensure errors are recorded in a useful, non-sensitive way.
For a short prototype, a simple background-task mechanism with polling is acceptable, provided it can handle the selected workflow. Document the limits of that approach and keep the processing layer modular enough to move into a dedicated worker later.

This Week's Theme: Side Hustle

Build something with open-weight models or open-source AI that someone would actually pay for.

That can mean running an open-weight model, building on an open-source agent harness or framework, running inference locally, or all three. Whatever you pick, the open pieces should be what makes your project work.

Think small, specific, and useful. The best builds here solve a real problem for a real person, whether that's a freelancer, a small business owner, a niche hobby community, or your friend who keeps complaining about the same tedious task. A few ideas to get you going:

An invoice and receipt sorter for freelancers that runs locally, so client data never leaves their laptop
A listing writer for Etsy or eBay sellers that turns a few product photos into a polished description
A menu translator for a local restaurant that handles their specific dishes better than a generic tool
In your post, tell us why open innovation matters for what you built. Does running your own model keep costs low enough that the business actually works? Keep your customers' data private? Let you fine-tune for a niche that a general-purpose API handles poorly? Avoid getting locked into someone else's pricing? Tell us where your open-based approach worked better than a closed one.

Bonus points if you land your first user. Get it in front of someone, let them use it, and tell us how it went. Did they pay? Would they? What did they ask for next?
